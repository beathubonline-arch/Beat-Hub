Before the modern world.

An illustrated-in-words journey into early human life, with careful distinctions between archaeological evidence and cinematic imagination.

Back to the channel hub ↗

1. The great timeline

Our ancestors did not suddenly wake up in a modern world. Human evolution involves many related populations and species over millions of years. Homo sapiens evolved in Africa by about 300,000 years ago. The emergence of farming and towns happened far later, at different times and in different places.

Remember: prehistoric means a time before written records for a particular society, not a time when people were less intelligent.

2. An evening around the fire

Fire made new things possible: cooking, warmth, light and sometimes nighttime social activity. Evidence for controlled fire differs by place and age, and archaeologists cannot reconstruct every detail of a single evening. Stories about songs, teaching, or conversations around ancient fires may be plausible but are not directly observable in the archaeological record.

3. Food without supermarkets

There never was a single universal Stone Age menu. People used locally available plants, animals, fish and shellfish in varied combinations. Teeth, stone tools, cut marks on bones, hearths and plant residues each offer incomplete but valuable clues about past diets.

4. Technologies that rarely survive

Stone artefacts are common in museums partly because stone survives exceptionally well. People also used wood, hides, fiber and other materials that decay more readily. This means the surviving record provides a biased sample of actual human ingenuity.

5. How did people find shelter?

Early humans inhabited many climates and landscapes. Evidence includes cave occupation, hearth distributions, post holes and other site features. A cinematic hut is an artistic reconstruction unless its specific design is supported by archaeological traces.

6. Before cities

For much of human history, people lived in small, mobile or seasonally moving groups. Farming developed independently in different world regions after the last Ice Age, with enormous regional variation. Settlement, agriculture and urbanization were extended processes rather than one global event.

7. Spot the evidence

  1. Direct evidence: excavated artefacts, fossil remains, datable layers and measurable residues.
  2. Interpretation: conclusions based on comparisons, models and context; interpretations may change.
  3. Reconstruction: pictures and scenes filling gaps. These can help learning, but they should never be presented as camera footage of the past.

Your three-question challenge

When a video says “ancient people always did this,” ask: What place and time period? What archaeological evidence supports it? Which details are educated guesses?

Further exploration: Browse exhibits and research introductions from the Smithsonian Human Origins Program, the Natural History Museum and university archaeology departments. Compare dates and interpretations as research develops.
